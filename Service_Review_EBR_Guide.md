# Service Review & Executive Business Review Guide
### Enterprise IT Operations | Monthly Reviews, QBRs, and Difficult Conversations

**Author:** Scott Boehler — Service Delivery & Operations Leader  
**Version:** 1.0  
**Last Updated:** May 2026  
**Applies To:** Service Delivery Managers, Account Managers, and Operations Leaders responsible for customer-facing service reviews across managed services, IT operations, and enterprise technology engagements

---

## Purpose & Scope

A service review is not a reporting exercise. It is a relationship management tool. Done right, it gives the customer confidence that someone is accountable for their service, that problems are being identified before they escalate, and that the provider is continuously improving. Done wrong, it is an hour of slides that nobody remembers and action items that disappear.

The difference between the two is almost always the Service Delivery Manager in the room.

This guide covers how to structure, prepare for, and run both monthly service reviews and quarterly business reviews — including what to do when the news is bad. Especially when the news is bad. How you handle a difficult service review tells the customer more about you than ten good ones ever will.

---

## Section 1 — Purpose of the Service Review

### What It Actually Is

A service review is a structured, recurring conversation between the provider and the customer that covers three things:

1. **How did we perform?** — Facts, metrics, SLA compliance. Not spin, not excuses.
2. **What are we working on?** — Open issues, action items, improvement initiatives.
3. **What is coming?** — Planned changes, upcoming risks, capacity considerations.

That is it. A service review that covers those three things honestly and efficiently is doing its job.

### What It Often Becomes — And Why That Is a Problem

In practice, service reviews often become one of two things — neither of which is useful.

The first is a victory lap. Green metrics on every slide, no acknowledgment of near-misses or trends, action items listed from last month with no update on whether they were actually completed. The customer leaves feeling like they were managed rather than served. They start to distrust the data.

The second is a defensive briefing. The SDM spends the meeting explaining why metrics were missed instead of what was done about it. Every red number has a paragraph of context designed to minimize it. The customer leaves feeling like the provider is more interested in protecting themselves than fixing the problem.

Neither builds the relationship. Both erode it.

The service review that works leads with honest data, acknowledges what went wrong without over-explaining it, states what was done about it, and gives the customer something concrete to hold the provider accountable to next month. That is the format this guide is built around.

### Monthly Review vs. Quarterly Business Review

These are different meetings with different purposes. Treating them the same is a common mistake.

| Aspect | Monthly Service Review | Quarterly Business Review (QBR) |
|---|---|---|
| **Purpose** | Operational performance — how did we do this month? | Strategic alignment — are we delivering value against business objectives? |
| **Duration** | 45–60 minutes | 90–120 minutes |
| **Audience** | Operational contacts — IT manager, service desk lead, operations team | Executive contacts — CIO, VP of IT, Director of Operations |
| **Agenda focus** | Metrics, incidents, SLA compliance, open action items | Business outcomes, roadmap, relationship health, strategic initiatives |
| **Tone** | Operational and factual | Strategic and forward-looking |
| **Frequency** | Monthly | Quarterly |
| **SDM role** | Leads and presents | Supports — executive sponsor may lead customer-facing portion |

A monthly service review that tries to be a QBR wastes everyone's time. A QBR that gets buried in monthly metrics misses the point entirely. Know which meeting you are in and run it accordingly.

## Section 2 — Audience and Stakeholder Management

### Getting the Right People in the Room

Wrong audience is one of the most common service review failures — and one of the most preventable. Before every service review, confirm who will be attending from both sides.

**Customer side — monthly review:**
- Primary IT contact or service desk manager
- Operations lead for the affected services
- Any open escalation owner on the customer side

**Customer side — QBR:**
- CIO or VP of IT — minimum
- Director of Operations or business unit lead
- Any executive sponsor named in the contract

**Provider side — monthly review:**
- Service Delivery Manager — owns the meeting
- Technical Account Manager or support lead if open technical issues exist
- Escalation manager if there are active escalations

**Provider side — QBR:**
- Service Delivery Manager
- Account Executive or Sales lead
- Executive Sponsor — VP or Director level
- Subject matter experts only if specific technical topics are on the agenda

### The Wrong People Problem

If the customer sends operational contacts to a QBR instead of executives, address it before the meeting — not during it. A QBR presented to the wrong audience produces no strategic alignment and wastes preparation time.

If the provider brings too many people, the customer feels outnumbered and the meeting loses focus. More than four provider contacts in a service review without a clear reason for each person signals poor preparation.

### The Escalation Contact Rule

If there is an active escalation with the customer — a missed SLA, an open complaint, a dissatisfied executive — make sure the right people from the provider side are in the room. Do not let an SDM walk into that meeting alone. They need internal backup, not because the SDM cannot handle it, but because showing up with the right level of seniority signals the provider takes it seriously.

## Section 3 — Service Review Cadence and Structure

### Monthly Service Review Agenda

**Total time: 45–60 minutes**

| Segment | Time | Owner | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Opening** | 5 min | SDM | Quick recap of last month's key themes — one sentence each. No slides needed. |
| **Performance Summary** | 15 min | SDM | SLA compliance, incident volume, MTTR, change success rate. Lead with the numbers — context comes after, not before. |
| **Open Issues & Escalations** | 10 min | SDM | Status of any open P1/P2 incidents, escalations, or known issues. Be specific — ticket numbers, current status, owner, ETA. |
| **Action Item Review** | 10 min | SDM | Every action item from last month reviewed. Completed, in progress, or overdue — no item left unaddressed. |
| **New Action Items** | 5 min | SDM | Items identified during this meeting. Owner and due date set before the meeting closes. |
| **Upcoming Activity** | 5 min | SDM | Planned changes, maintenance windows, any risk to next month's performance. |
| **Close** | 5 min | SDM | Confirm action items, confirm next meeting date. |

### Quarterly Business Review Agenda

**Total time: 90–120 minutes**

| Segment | Time | Owner | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Executive Opening** | 10 min | Executive Sponsor (provider) | Relationship context, strategic priorities, what the provider is focused on for the customer |
| **Quarterly Performance Summary** | 20 min | SDM | Three months of performance data. Trends, not just snapshots. What improved, what did not. |
| **SLA Compliance & Incident Review** | 15 min | SDM | Detailed SLA performance. Any breach — full explanation, root cause, corrective action. |
| **Open Issues & Escalation Status** | 10 min | SDM | Any open items from the quarter. Nothing carries over without a status. |
| **Customer Feedback & Satisfaction** | 10 min | Customer lead | Customer's view of the service. What is working, what is not. Provider listens — does not defend. |
| **Strategic Roadmap** | 20 min | Account Executive / SDM | What is coming — new features, platform updates, improvement initiatives. Tie to customer's business objectives. |
| **Action Items & Next Steps** | 10 min | SDM | All action items from the meeting assigned with owners and due dates. |
| **Close** | 5 min | Executive Sponsor (provider) | Thank you, commitment to follow-through, next QBR date confirmed. |

## Section 4 — The Metrics That Belong in a Service Review

### What to Show

Service reviews should present the metrics the customer actually cares about — not every metric available in the reporting system. More data is not more useful. More relevant data is.

**Monthly review — core metrics:**

| Metric | What It Shows | How to Present It |
|---|---|---|
| SLA Compliance Rate | Are we meeting our commitments? | Percentage — with target. Green if at or above target, red if below. No ambiguity. |
| Incident Volume by Severity | How many issues did the customer experience? | Month over month trend — not just this month's number |
| MTTR by Severity | How fast are we resolving issues? | Actual vs. target — with trend direction |
| Change Success Rate | Are changes being implemented cleanly? | Percentage of changes with no incident or rollback |
| Open Action Items | Are we following through? | Count of open items, count overdue — both numbers matter |
| Customer-Reported Issues | What is the customer experiencing that may not be in the ticket system? | Verbal — ask the customer directly, do not assume the ticket count tells the whole story |

**QBR — additional metrics:**

| Metric | What It Shows |
|---|---|
| Quarterly SLA trend | Are we improving, declining, or flat over three months? |
| Major incident count and resolution time | Sev 1 and Sev 2 performance specifically |
| CSAT score | Customer satisfaction trend |
| PIR completion rate | Are we learning from failures? |
| Roadmap milestone status | Are strategic commitments being met? |

### What to Leave Out

Do not fill a service review with metrics that neither party will act on. Reporting 47 different KPIs in a monthly review signals that no one prioritized what actually matters. Pick the eight to ten metrics that directly reflect service quality and present those consistently every month.

Consistency matters as much as accuracy. If the metrics change every month, the customer cannot track trends and loses confidence in the data.

### How to Present a Metric That Is Below Target

Lead with the number. Do not bury it at the end, do not surround it with positive metrics to soften the blow, and do not introduce it with three paragraphs of context before the customer even knows what you are talking about.

> "SLA compliance for P2 incidents this month was 87% against a 95% target. Here is what drove that, what we found, and what we have done."

Then cover three things — in this order:
1. What caused the miss — specifically, not generally
2. What the root cause was — what process or system gap allowed it to happen
3. What corrective action has been implemented — not planned, implemented

If the corrective action has not been implemented yet, say so and give a specific date. Do not present a plan as if it is already a fix.

## Section 5 — Handling Bad News the Right Way

This is the section most service review guides skip. It should be the longest one.

### The Principle

When something went wrong — an SLA breach, a major incident, a missed commitment — the customer already knows. They lived through it. What they do not know is whether the provider understands what happened, takes it seriously, and has done something about it.

The only question in their mind walking into that meeting is: are they going to be honest with me, or are they going to try to manage me?

Be honest. Every time.

Honest does not mean defensive. It does not mean over-explaining. It means stating the facts, owning the outcome, explaining the cause, and presenting the corrective action. That sequence — in that order — is what rebuilds trust after a failure.

### Internal Alignment Before the Meeting

No executive on the provider side should walk into a difficult service review without knowing what is in it. This is not about getting your story straight — it is about making sure the right people have visibility and are prepared to support the SDM if the conversation escalates.

The internal pre-brief (covered in Section 6) is not optional when bad news is on the agenda. Surprises in front of the customer damage the provider's credibility. A provider team that looks aligned and prepared — even when delivering bad news — signals accountability. A provider team that is visibly misaligned in front of the customer signals chaos.

### The Bad News Framework

When presenting a missed SLA, a major incident, or any other negative outcome:

**1. State the fact first.**
> "In March, we had two P1 incidents that exceeded our 4-hour resolution SLA. The first resolved in 5 hours and 42 minutes. The second resolved in 6 hours and 11 minutes."

No preamble. No softening. The fact first.

**2. Own it.**
> "These were misses. We did not meet the commitment."

Do not qualify it. "We almost met it" or "it was close to target" is not owning it — it is minimizing it. The customer knows when a miss is a miss.

**3. Explain the cause — specifically.**
> "Both incidents involved a third-party carrier outage that extended our resolution timeline. In the first incident, our vendor escalation was delayed by 40 minutes because the on-call contact list had not been updated after a personnel change on the vendor's side. In the second incident, the root cause was the same gap — the contact list."

One cause, specific. Not "there were a number of contributing factors." Not "it was a complex situation." What actually happened.

**4. State what was done — not what will be done.**
> "We have updated the vendor escalation contact list and added a quarterly review of all vendor on-call contacts to our change management process. The first quarterly review is scheduled for April 15."

If the fix is not yet implemented, say so and give a date:
> "The contact list has been updated. The quarterly review process is being documented now — it will be in place by April 15."

**5. Do not over-explain.**
Make the point. Stop talking. If the customer has questions, they will ask. Continuing to explain after you have covered the four steps above signals anxiety, not accountability.

### When the Customer Is Angry

If the customer comes into the meeting already frustrated — escalation in the room, raised voices, pointed questions — do not match their energy and do not become defensive.

Let them finish. Give them the floor first. Every word they say is information about what matters to them and what they need to hear.

Then respond with facts. Not apologies that go on for three minutes, not promises that cannot be backed up, not deflections to third parties. Facts, cause, action taken.

> "You're right. That should not have happened. Here is what caused it and here is what we have done."

That is the entire response. Everything else is noise.

### What Not to Do

- Do not blame the customer's infrastructure or systems unless you have documented proof and the contract supports it — and even then, lead with the outcome, not the deflection
- Do not blame vendors in a way that makes the provider look like a victim — the customer hired the provider, not the vendor
- Do not promise something you cannot deliver to get through the meeting
- Do not let internal team members contradict each other in front of the customer — if there is disagreement, it goes to the internal pre-brief, not the customer meeting
- Do not minimize a miss with comparisons — "99% of our customers had no issues" is not a comfort to the customer sitting in front of you who did

## Section 6 — The Internal Pre-Brief

No one on the provider side walks into a service review — especially a difficult one — without a pre-brief. This is non-negotiable.

### What the Pre-Brief Covers

The internal pre-brief happens 24–48 hours before the customer meeting. Fifteen to thirty minutes. The SDM leads it.

**Agenda:**

1. **What is on the agenda?** Walk through every slide or topic. No surprises in the room.
2. **What is the red news?** Name every metric that is below target, every open escalation, every action item that is overdue. The team needs to know what is coming before the customer does.
3. **What are the answers?** For every red item — root cause, corrective action, current status. Everyone in the room needs to be aligned on the answer before the meeting starts.
4. **Who presents what?** Assign segments. The SDM does not have to present everything — but the SDM owns the meeting and is the escalation point if something goes sideways.
5. **What decisions might come up?** If there are open issues that could require a decision — a service credit discussion, a contract change, a scope question — who has the authority to make that decision, and what is the position going in?

### Why It Matters

A provider team that walks into a customer meeting misaligned — where one person says one thing and another contradicts it — has already lost the meeting. The customer's confidence in the provider's ability to manage their service is directly correlated with how well the provider's team manages themselves.

The pre-brief is also where the SDM can flag if they need executive support. If the customer has escalated to senior leadership, the SDM should not be in that room alone. That is not a sign of weakness — it is a sign that the SDM understands the relationship dynamics and is protecting the account.

## Section 7 — Action Item Discipline

Action items are where service reviews either build trust or destroy it. A customer who watches an action item carry over from month to month with no progress stops believing anything the provider commits to.

### The Rules

**Every action item must have three things before the meeting closes:**
- Specific description of what will be done — not "review the process" but "update the vendor escalation contact list by April 15"
- Named owner — one person, not a team
- Due date — specific calendar date

**Every action item from last month is reviewed at the start of the current month's meeting.** Completed items are acknowledged and closed. In-progress items get a status and a revised date if needed. Overdue items get an explanation and a new committed date — in front of the customer.

Do not hide overdue items in the middle of a long action item list. If something is overdue, address it directly — same framework as bad news. State the fact, own it, explain the cause, give the new date.

### Tracking Action Items

Action items from service reviews are tracked in ServiceNow — not in a spreadsheet, not in a Word document, not in the meeting notes from last month that nobody can find. Every action item is a task record linked to the customer account.

This serves two purposes. First, it creates an audit trail that both the customer and the provider can reference. Second, it prevents the action item from disappearing after the meeting — it is assigned in the system, and the system will not let you forget it.

### The Test

The test of your action item discipline is simple: if a customer asked you right now to pull up every open action item from the last six months across every service review, could you do it in under two minutes?

If not — the system is broken and the customer knows it before you do.

## Section 8 — Service Review Preparation Checklist

Use this checklist before every service review. If any item is not confirmed, the meeting is not ready.

### Data and Metrics
- [ ] All metrics pulled from ServiceNow for the correct reporting period
- [ ] Month-over-month trend data available — not just this month's snapshot
- [ ] SLA compliance calculated and verified — no manual adjustments without documentation
- [ ] Incident volume and MTTR by severity confirmed
- [ ] Change success rate confirmed
- [ ] CSAT scores pulled if available

### Open Items
- [ ] All open action items from last meeting reviewed — completed, in progress, or overdue status confirmed
- [ ] All open escalations have a current status and named owner
- [ ] Any open P1/P2 incidents have documented root cause and corrective action

### Bad News Preparation
- [ ] Every metric below target has a root cause and corrective action ready
- [ ] Every overdue action item has an explanation and a new committed date
- [ ] Internal pre-brief completed — all attendees aligned on red items and answers

### Logistics
- [ ] Correct attendees confirmed on both sides — right people, right level for this meeting type
- [ ] Meeting materials sent to customer contact 24 hours in advance (QBR only)
- [ ] Executive sponsor briefed if escalation or difficult conversation expected
- [ ] Action item tracker up to date in ServiceNow

### Closing Readiness
- [ ] Next meeting date confirmed and on calendar
- [ ] Action item template ready — owner and due date captured for every new item before the meeting closes

## Section 9 — Common Service Review Mistakes

These are the patterns that erode customer confidence over time. Some happen once and get corrected. Others become habits that define how the customer sees the provider.

| Mistake | What It Looks Like | What It Costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Presenting data without context** | SLA compliance shown as a number with no explanation of what drove it | Customer cannot distinguish a one-time miss from a trend — loses confidence in the data |
| **Burying bad news** | Red metrics shown last, surrounded by green ones | Customer notices. It looks like the provider is hoping they will not. |
| **Action items that disappear** | Last month's action items not reviewed — new ones added | Customer stops believing anything the provider commits to |
| **Over-explaining a miss** | Three minutes of context before admitting the SLA was missed | Signals anxiety and defensiveness — not accountability |
| **Showing up unprepared** | SDM does not know the numbers, cannot answer basic questions | Single fastest way to lose a customer's confidence — they were more prepared for this meeting than you were |
| **Wrong people in the room** | Monthly ops review presented to C-suite, or QBR presented to operational contacts | Wrong level of conversation for wrong audience — nothing lands |
| **No internal pre-brief** | Provider team contradicts each other in front of the customer | Customer loses confidence in the provider's ability to manage their own team, let alone the service |
| **Promising to get back to them** | Every difficult question met with "I will follow up on that" | One or two is fine. A pattern of deferral signals the SDM does not know the service well enough. |
| **Generic slides every month** | Same deck, same format, different numbers dropped in | Customer stops reading it. The review becomes a compliance exercise rather than a conversation. |
| **Not asking the customer how they feel** | Entire meeting is provider presenting — customer never asked for their perspective | Misses the most important signal in the room — what the customer actually thinks of the service |

- [ ] Numbers confirmed — no surprises in the data

- [ ] Right people in the room

1. State the fact first — no preamble
2. Own it — no qualifications
3. Explain the cause — specifically
4. State what was done — not what will be done
